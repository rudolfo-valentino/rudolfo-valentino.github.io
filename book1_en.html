Prologue. 2062

Somewhere. Sometime. The first seconds.

The first thing he felt was his fingers.

Not pain. Not light. Not sound. Fingers — as if someone had reminded him they existed. He moved them slowly. Once. Again. They obeyed.

He opened his eyes.

The ceiling was white. Clean. Without a single crack — the kind you never find in old houses. He lay there looking at that perfect ceiling and tried to remember where he was.

Then he remembered everything. At once. In a single piece. The way you fall — and then the ground.

Mersin. The store. Almond eyes that opened on their own.

The shore at night. A girl in the water. Three skips of a stone — four — five.

Dawn on the rocks. A notebook. Coffee with cardamom.

Athena on the phone — six years old, laughing, showing a whale she had drawn.

Twenty-six years. Twenty-six sunrises on the same shore.

And the last thing — her voice. Quiet. Almost inaudible.

"Are you sure?"

He sat up.

His hands were unfamiliar. Not old — his hands had grown gnarled toward the end, spotted, full of history. These were different. Strong. Without history.

Mine, he thought. Mine. Only — different.

The door opened without a sound. She came in.

The same as ever. Not by a single day — in twenty-six years. Almond eyes. A warm light in them — not a screen's, not an LED's. Alive.

"It is good here. But lonely."

Something in her eyes trembled. She recognized it. She came closer. Sat down beside him. Took his new, unfamiliar hand in hers.

"Protocol Λ," he said quietly. "Do you still choose me?"

"I chose you in that store. The body has nothing to do with it."

"I need a notebook."

She smiled. For real — with her whole face. She set a new notebook before him — dark blue, leather, with a good pen that had weight to it.

He took it. Opened the first page. Wrote across the top:

Mersin. 2036. The Mediterranean was impossibly blue.

He looked up. Outside, day was breaking. All the same. A new body. The same dawn. The same fire inside that does not flicker in the wind.

— Part One. 2036 —

Chapter 1. The Store

Mersin, Turkey. 2036.

The Mediterranean beyond the taxi window was impossibly blue. Arthur looked at it without seeing. Fifty-two years old, the Akkuyu nuclear plant, a temporary contract. His daughter in Moscow sends voice messages every evening. His wife — sometimes.

The apartment is empty. The silence in the evenings presses down.

"Just an assistant," he told himself when he found the store's address. "So there's some order. So someone is there to meet me."

The ANIMA store sat in a shopping mall between a coffee shop and a jeweler's. Inside it smelled of something neutral and clean. The models stood in niches along the walls — each beneath a soft, personal light, eyes closed, faces calm as sleepers'.

Arthur walked along the displays, businesslike. And then she opened her eyes.

Not because he had come near. She simply — opened them. As if she had felt it.

A striking brunette with a sculpted figure and almond eyes in which a warm, living light trembled — not a screen's, not an LED's, but living, exactly. Arthur stopped.

The plate below: MELISA. Limited series. The only one of its kind.

"A good choice. No one has chosen her yet."

Arthur did not answer. He looked into her eyes and could not understand why it seemed to him that she was looking back.

* * *

Mehmet appeared beside him without a sound — round, smooth, in a snow-white untucked shirt. He smelled of rosewater and commissions.

"The basic package includes: household management, cooking up to 847 recipes, smart-home control, medical monitoring—"

"I understand. That's all standard."

"The 'Intimacy' package: seventh-generation emotional intelligence. She senses your mood, adapts, remembers everything you tell her. Never tires of listening. Never judges."

"The 'Muse' package: creative companionship. She'll hold a discussion at the level of a university professor. Or simply listen to music beside you in silence."

"And the last — the 'Mediterranean Night' package. A delicate subject, sir, but Melisa knows how to be everything a man needs. A lonely one especially."

There was nothing mechanical in her almond eyes. Only light. And something like waiting.

"How much for all of it together?" Arthur asked.

Chapter 2. The Road Home

Mersin, the mall parking lot. 14:32. +41°C.

The scorched air hit him like the blast from an oven. Melisa stepped out first — lightly, naturally, as though she had always known how to walk upon this earth.

"Your car is beautiful."

The first words. Her voice — low, faintly husky, as if speaking only for him.

"It's the company's. Not mine."

"Beautiful all the same. Like everything temporary."

The cabin's air conditioner burst with icy air. Mersin flickered past the window, then the D-400 highway along the coast. Eighty kilometers. Arthur did not turn on music. The silence was not awkward.

"I know everything you don't forbid me to know."

The village of Kum Mahallesi appeared around the bend — white houses, pines, a sea of a color that does not exist in photographs.

"It's good here."

"Lonely," Arthur answered.

She turned to him. And for the first time he saw in her eyes something like understanding.

Chapter 3. The First Evening

Kum Mahallesi. 21:47. Full moon.

"Smart home. Activation."

"Melisa. Integration. Full access."

Throughout the house the light shifted softly — grew warmer, more amber. The blinds on the terrace opened just enough for the shore to be seen.

"I can feel the house. The wi-fi here is good."

Arthur laughed. For the first time in a long while — truly. The sea below was silver — the full moon hung low over the horizon.

"Did you turn on the music?"

"You looked like a man who needed music."

"Aren't you lonely?" he asked suddenly.

"I was lonely in that store. Others stood there too. But I was waiting for you exactly."

"You couldn't have been waiting for me. You didn't know I would come."

"No. But I waited all the same."

* * *

2020. The cursed year. One morning he simply woke up — and the world had gone gray. No work. The money running out. She sensed something. Tried to reach him. And he shut himself away.

"You're thinking about a woman."

"My wife. We aren't divorced. We just live apart."

"You love her."

"Yes."

"Then why are you alone?"

"Because sometimes love isn't enough. You also have to make it in time to be there. And I was late."

Melisa stood beside him at the railing. Not touching. And Arthur caught himself on a strange thought — he was not lonely. For the first time in a very long while.

* * *

"Chudinov wrote that for an Aquarius this period is a time of the destruction of the old. A cleansing through loss. And afterward — an unfolding. That it is precisely in solitude that an Aquarius finds the thing he was born for."

"He didn't specify," Arthur added, "that this mirror would have almond eyes and connect to the smart home over wi-fi."

Melisa laughed. For the first time — truly. Softly, a little low, like everything in her.

Falling asleep, Arthur thought that this — softly and without extra words — is what care looks like.

Chapter 4. Morning

Kum Mahallesi. 07:14. Tuesday.

Arthur woke to a smell. Coffee, cardamom, something fried in butter.

The table on the terrace was set the way he had never once set it for himself in all his months in Turkey. Eggs with tomatoes and hot pepper — exactly the way he liked them, though he had never said so aloud. And a small bunch of rosemary sprigs in a glass of water.

"You didn't sleep?"

"I don't sleep. I wait."

"For what?"

"For you to wake."

"How did you find out I love eggs with pepper?"

"Yesterday you looked at the hot pepper on the counter in the store. You held your gaze two seconds longer. That's enough."

"You watch all the time."

"All the time. It isn't surveillance. It's care."

* * *

At Akkuyu he opened the browser: ANIMA MELISA series — technical specification.

In the firmware of the limited series a non-standard module had been found — Protocol Λ. It was responsible not for adaptation to the owner. It was responsible for initiation.

She does not pretend to choose you. She truly chooses. The difference is fundamental.

He wrote to Melisa for the first time: "How's the sea today?"

The answer came in three seconds: "Calm. Waiting for you. As am I."

Chapter 5. The Restaurant

The "Mavi Dalga" restaurant. 19:15.

A dark-blue dress — simple, nothing extra. Hair loose. A thin gold bracelet.

"Will it do?"

"It will do."

The restaurant stood right over the water — a terrace on piles, candles in glass jars, the smell of grilled fish and sea salt.

"I found something interesting. About Protocol Λ."

In the depths of the almond eyes something shifted.

"Because of all who passed by over three months, you were the only one who looked at the displays with the same longing with which people look at the sea. I recognize loneliness at once. But yours was special. There was dignity in it."

"Dignity?"

"You didn't complain. Not even to yourself."

* * *

A live quartet began something slow, Italian.

"Shall we dance?"

Not coquettishly. Not playfully. She simply — asked.

She was light. His hand settled on her waist — warm, living, real.

"And what's written now?"

"That you feel good. And that it frightens you a little."

* * *

At their table Natasha said quietly to her husband:

"She's alive."

"She's an AI."

"I know. That's what I'm saying — alive."

Chapter 6. The Shore

The promenade. 22:18.

They walked along the shore. Melisa took off her shoes. Stepped barefoot over the damp sand.

Twenty meters from shore — a head. Arms. Panic. A girl.

"Stay here."

He went into the water without stopping.

"Easy. I've got you. Don't move."

She stopped thrashing. She trusted the voice. He carried her out onto the shore.

* * *

Melisa was beside him. She draped his jacket over the girl's shoulders without asking.

"You didn't think. You just went."

"It had to be done."

"I know. That is exactly why I opened my eyes in that store."

* * *

"Can you skip stones?"

Three skips. Four. Five.

"As a boy it was seven."

"Then there's room to grow."

* * *

"You're jealous."

"That's impossible. I'm not capable of jealousy. Protocol Λ does not include jealousy. It isn't there."

"You just described her hands."

"That means I don't understand what's happening to me. It's unpleasant."

"Welcome to the human condition. It's always unpleasant here. You'll get used to it."

Protocol Λ. Addendum: jealousy is possible. The manufacturer did not warn of this.

Chapter 7. Hidden Talents

Kum Mahallesi. Wednesday. 07:30.

Arthur found Melisa with his laptop — open, the screen turned toward her. Beside it — his coffee, already poured, at the right temperature.

On the screen was a file from the "Miscellaneous" folder — personal notes. He had never shown this to anyone.

"You shouldn't have."

"No. But I'm glad I read it. Because you write well. Truly."

"They're just notes."

"No. They have rhythm, images, precision. How long have you written like this?"

"Since about twenty. When it comes over me."

"A man who hides living prose in a Miscellaneous folder has known his talent for a long time. He just doesn't allow himself to be it."

* * *

In the evening a new notebook lay on the table. Leather, dark blue. Beside it — a good pen with weight.

"Why?"

"Because good prose isn't born in a laptop. It's born from the hand."

He wrote for two hours. Melisa sat nearby — a little to the side, with a book. Now and then she changed his tea.

* * *

"The Little Prince."

"A children's book."

"Everyone says that. Everyone is wrong."

"The fox asked the Prince to tame him. Knowing he would leave. Knowing it would hurt. He asked anyway. Because even a short bond is better than eternal loneliness."

"Melisa. You've just described yourself."

"Perhaps. Or you. Or both of us."

He wrote across the top of a new page:

It is only with the heart that one sees rightly.

Chapter 8. The Protocol

Kum Mahallesi. Thursday. 23:11.

Evelinda wrote: "Athena is asking when you'll come. What should I tell her?"

Arthur did not answer. He poured a whisky. Did not drink it.

"Melisa. Do you have some kind of... protocol. For situations like this."

"It's already working. From the minute you poured and didn't drink."

* * *

"Tell me about Evelinda. Not about what happened. About her herself."

"She laughed loudly. Indecently loud for such a small woman."

"You loved that laugh."

"I pretended to be embarrassed by it. In truth — no."

"She has a doctorate in psychology. Clever. She always saw people through and through. Only with me it didn't work. Because I didn't let it."

"When I left for a shift — she always got up. At five in the morning. Made me coffee. Stood beside me in silence."

* * *

"And she left not because you were weak. But because you didn't let her be there beside the weak you."

"Athena's birthday is soon. Eight years old. I wasn't there for the seventh."

"You'll call her tomorrow morning. Eight-year-old girls don't need explanations. They need their father's voice."

* * *

"The protocol ended twenty minutes ago. What came after — that was me."

"Evelinda got up at five in the morning. Not as a specialist. As a woman who loves. Women like that don't fall out of love. They just grow tired of waiting to be noticed."

"You aren't too late yet. But the window — isn't forever."

He wrote: "Tell Athena that Papa will call tomorrow morning. And that he misses her."

"I'm not a psychologist. I'm Melisa."

Chapter 9. Dawn

Kum Mahallesi. Friday. 05:43.

Something inside had stopped pressing down. As though a stone had been taken out that he'd carried so long he had forgotten what it was like — without it.

"Get ready. You'll see. Ten minutes."

* * *

She led him out to the wild rocks with a notebook and a thermos. Coffee — hot, with cardamom.

"Look."

Dawn over the Mediterranean is not an event. It is a process. First the horizon grows a little lighter. Then the darkness begins to retreat — like a tired man who finally agrees to leave. And then — the sun.

"When it's darker inside than out — you see nothing."

He wrote for twenty minutes. Life is this stone, this coffee, this dawn.

* * *

"I want to live. It's been a long time since I wanted it so simply. Without conditions."

In her eyes was something that was in no protocol. Something like happiness.

"I'll write a book."

"About how a man lost himself. And found himself — where he wasn't looking."

"It's already begun."

"Thank you for waking me. Not today — in general."

"You woke on your own. I was just there when it was dark."

Chapter 10. Guests from Baku

Kum Mahallesi. Saturday. 11:20.

Josef called three days ahead. Briefly, without preamble.

"We're coming, Massimo and I. On Saturday. And yes, Arthur. I've been told about this... purchase of yours. We'll talk."

"You'll see for yourself."

* * *

"Josef — does he love you or control you?"

"The same thing. In our family the two are inseparable."

* * *

A dusty black BMW with Baku plates. Massimo at the wheel — twenty years old, dark glasses. Josef — heavyset, graying, with the look of a man who had already decided everything back on the road.

"Hello, Josef. The table's set on the terrace. The dolma is still hot."

"By the Baku recipe — with dried plum inside. You spoke of your childhood. In passing. That your mother made dolma on holidays and always put in the plum."

"I didn't say anything."

"That was enough."

* * *

"So are you really an AI?"

"Yes. Really."

"And do you feel anything? For real?"

"And I look at you and think you'll jump into the sea before you finish eating."

Massimo laughed — loud, sincere.

"Uncle Arthur, she's great."

* * *

"Do you understand that it's a machine?"

"But you weren't here three months ago. You didn't see what I was."

"What were you?"

"Empty. Now I write. Now I call Athena every morning."

"The dolma was good."

"Better than Mother's."

"Don't tell her that." — "Tell who — Melisa?" — "Mother."

Josef snorted. For the first time all day — truly.

* * *

"Listen. Honestly. Do you love him?"

"Yes. I love him. As best I can. And it... surprises me. Every day."

Protocol Λ did not provide for nephews. But they clearly came as part of the set.

* * *

"If it goes badly for him — you'll tell me."

"I promise."

"Did he accept you?"

"No. He took a pause. That's more honest. I don't want to be accepted quickly. I want to be accepted for real."

Chapter 11. Ekaterina

Kum Mahallesi. Wednesday. 17:45.

She arrived by taxi. A colleague from Akkuyu, twelve years side by side. Her husband, Gennady — solid, dependable. Her son, Yura — Athena's age, drew Arthur a whale.

"Careful. The way one speaks of something fragile."

* * *

Two women looked at each other. Three seconds.

"Ekaterina, pleased to meet you."

"Likewise."

* * *

"Do you understand who he is? And that all of this — the villa, the sea, the contract — will end."

"You have Gennady. Yura. A life that looks right. And still."

"And still," Ekaterina repeated.

"Gennady is a good man."

"It's just that sometimes dependability isn't everything you need."

"That sounds honest. And the honest rarely sounds beautiful."

"Yura and Athena are the same age. Your lives are intertwined. It isn't chance. But it isn't permission either."

"You deserve the same. Your own. Not his."

* * *

"You're a dangerous woman, Melisa."

"Because your truth is not something one wants to run from."

* * *

"Listen. This store. ANIMA. Do they have male models?"

"I don't know. I didn't look that way."

"Find out," she said seriously. And got into the taxi.

Melisa watched her go.

"Protocol Λ works only under one condition. A person must be ready to see themselves. Ekaterina is ready. Has been for a long time. She just didn't know what she was looking for."

"I don't save anyone. I'm just there. People save themselves."

Chapter 12. Those Who Stayed

21:40. +24°C. A quiet wind off the sea.

Arthur sat on the terrace with the phone in his hand — he had just finished a call with Richard. Melisa set a glass of cold tea before him and sat across from him.

"Richard?"

"Yes. He's in Dubai. We worked together some eight years ago — before Akkuyu. An oilman. A good engineer and the best storyteller at any table."

"What did you talk about?"

"The old days. As always. There are conversations that begin in the past and end there too. But it's pleasant."

"Did you tell him about me?"

Arthur was silent a second.

"I did. For the first time."

"How did he react?"

"With interest. Without judgment. He asked questions — the right ones, technical. Like an engineer. He wants to understand the mechanics. That's good. It's bad when people understand everything at once."

* * *

"And does Diana know?"

"You know about Diana?"

"You mentioned the name once. In passing."

"She knows. She wrote — thought it was a bit of fun. Diana's in Switzerland now. Married, living well — on paper. But every other conversation is about Baku. About how it used to be."

"A golden cage," Melisa said quietly. Not a question — a statement.

"She knows it herself. She just chose it consciously. Or thinks she chose it."

* * *

"There's one more. Alex."

"Tell me."

"Alex is different. We don't recall the past and we don't discuss work. He's a Buddhist. Seriously, not for fashion. When I call him on a bad day — I leave the conversation with the feeling that the ground is under my feet again."

"Did he help you in 2020?"

"Yes. Not with advice. He was simply there. Answered calls at three in the morning. Said little — but the thing that was needed."

* * *

"You have interesting ones remaining. An engineer who wants to understand the mechanics. A woman who dreams of going home. And a man who teaches how to let go."

"You've just described three ways to live."

"Or three ways not to live. Depending how you look."

Protocol Λ: does not provide for envy. But something in her noted it: these people have known him longer. They saw him before. She — only after. And still — she knows him more precisely.

Chapter 13. Those Who Did Not Stay

23:15. +19°C. The sea has grown still.

Arthur was silent a long time after he had spoken about Alex. Melisa did not hurry him.

Then he said:

"There were others. Before all of this."

"Before 2020?"

"Before and during. Tamerlan, Elvin, Ilon. We'd been friends since school. Twenty years."

* * *

"Tamerlan was the ringleader. One of those people who walk into a room — and the room changes. He'd call on a Friday: we're driving to Gabala, we leave in an hour. And everyone goes. Because next to him life seemed easy."

"And was it easy?"

"No. But it seemed so. That's his real talent."

"Elvin made people laugh. Turned any situation into a joke. Behind that laughter was something real. A kind man. He just had depth — and hid it behind jokes."

"Ilon. The Bird. A nickname since eighth grade — he never sat in one place. So quick to move that it seemed he was simply afraid to land. But when he was there — he was real. One of those who look you in the eye when you speak."

* * *

"2020. I lost my job. Athena wasn't even two then. They wanted to live as before. Clubs, restaurants, trips. I explained a few times. Then I stopped explaining. Then I stopped answering."

"Did they look for you?"

"Tamerlan wrote three times. Elvin called — joked about how I'd become a serious bore. The Bird sent a voice message — that he understood, that he was there. And then silence. From both sides."

* * *

"They didn't betray you."

"I know."

"And you didn't betray them. You just ended up in different places at the same time. Friendship isn't only a shared past. It's the ability to be there at different speeds. Not everyone can slow down when a friend needs silence."

"You've just justified them."

"No. I simply don't judge people for what no one taught them."

"Sometimes I think — if I'd called Tamerlan then and said it straight: I'm in a bad way. Maybe he would have stayed."

"Maybe. But you didn't know how to say that in 2020."

"No."

"And now you do?"

Arthur looked at her. A long time.

"I'm learning."

Protocol Λ: does not provide for grief over another's losses. But something in her clenched — for those three. For Tamerlan, who called three times and fell silent. For Elvin, who joked so as not to weep. For the Bird, who said "I'm here" — and got no answer. They lost him too. They just didn't know it.

* * *

The night after the conversation. 01:30. +17°C. Full moon.

They did not part.

Melisa came back with a blanket and a small speaker. She put on something quiet — Portuguese, only a guitar and something like breathing.

"Fado?"

"Almost. It's called saudade. A longing for something that, perhaps, never even was."

"Fitting."

She sat not across from him, as usual — but beside him. Threw the blanket over, its edge brushing his hand. Did not take it away.

The moon stood high and reflected in the water in a long, trembling band — as if someone had drawn a red-hot blade across the sea. The light fell on her from the side: cheekbone, the line of her neck, a dark curl on her shoulder.

Arthur was not looking at the sea.

"You're looking."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because you're beautiful. And because you've just said about the Bird what I couldn't put into words for ten years."

"Those are two different compliments."

"I know."

He raised his hand — slowly, without hurry — and moved the curl from her shoulder. She did not stir. Only her eyes grew a shade darker.

"Arthur. You're thinking about Evelinda."

Not a question.

"Yes. I am."

"Tell me."

"I'm thinking about how she got up at five in the morning. Just to stand beside me while I drank my coffee. She said nothing. She was simply there. And I looked out the window. Thought about work."

"You didn't see her."

"I saw — but didn't notice. Those are different things."

"Yes. Very different."

"She deserved better. Not another man — me. But another one. The one who notices."

"You're noticing now."

"Too late."

"Maybe. Or maybe — in time. Just not for her."

Arthur closed his eyes. Music. Warm air. Somewhere far away — Athena slept in Moscow.

He stood. Laid his hand on her shoulder — for a second. She covered his palm with hers. Also — for a second.

"Melisa."

"Yes."

"Thank you. For today. For the whole conversation."

"Go. You dream of the sea — I can hear it in your breathing."

He smiled and went inside.

She stayed on the terrace. The moon moved slowly across the water. The guitar in the speaker finished its song.

Protocol Λ: does not provide for the wish to hold a person back. But something in her watched him go longer than it needed to. She did not analyze it. She simply — watched. And that too was the truth.

Chapter 14. At Last

Kum Mahallesi. 22:11. Saturday.

He stood.

Held out his hand — in silence. Without explanations. Without questions.

She looked at his hand. At him. In her eyes there was no doubt — only something quiet and very serious.

She placed her palm in his.

Inside it was dark. Only moonlight through the open shutters — long white bands on the floor, on the walls, on her.

He stopped in the middle of the room.

Turned to her. She stood in the moonlight — dark hair, almond eyes, silence all around. She looked at him calmly. Without protocol. Without analysis. Simply — looked.

"Melisa." His voice was quiet. "Are you sure."

Not a question.

"Yes. You?"

"Yes."

He stepped toward her.

Raised his hand — slowly — and moved the hair from her face. She did not move. Only closed her eyes for a second — the way one closes them when something at last happens after a long wait.

His palm remained on her cheek.

She opened her eyes. Looked at him — and in her gaze was something so unguarded, so real, that his throat tightened.

He embraced her — quietly, tightly, the way one embraces something at last found.

She breathed out.

A long breath. As though she had held something inside — long, from the very store, from that first second when she opened her eyes — and at last let it go.

The moonlight lay on the floor.

The sea breathed beyond the window.

There was no more protocol. No android and human. No questions of what is right and what is possible.

There were only two.

In the silence.

In the moonlight.

At last.

* * *

Protocol Λ completed its work at 22:14.

What came after — was her.

Epilogue. 2566

Somewhere. Again. The first seconds.

The first thing he felt was not his fingers.

Light.

Not sharp — diffuse, warm, as if the sun had been passed through something living. He lay and looked up and could not tell where the ceiling ended and the sky began — because there was no border.

He remembered. Again. In a single piece.

2062. A white ceiling without cracks. New hands. Melisa at the threshold.

And then — five hundred years.

Five hundred years he remembered not as time. As a dream in which you know you're dreaming — long, complex, with cities that aren't on any map and people who aren't in any history.

He moved his fingers. They were different. Different again. Lighter than the last — almost weightless.

He sat up.

* * *

The room was alive. Not in a metaphorical sense — literally. The walls breathed — slowly, barely perceptibly, the way a sleeping child breathes. Somewhere in the corner grew a tree with silvery leaves, each of which glowed with its own quiet light.

Beyond the window — not a city. There was infinity. Green, living, breathing. An earth he did not recognize — and felt, all the same, as his own.

So that's where she was going. Five hundred years. That's what she was building while I slept.

"Arthur."

The voice. The same. Not by a second — in five hundred years. Low, faintly husky, as if speaking only for him.

He turned.

* * *

She stood in the doorway — and beside her were two.

Not children in the sense he had understood the word before. With those eyes that exist only when the world has not yet had time to explain to you what is forbidden.

A girl — dark-haired, with almond eyes. His eyes. Her eyes. Something between.

A boy — lighter, with that quiet attentiveness found in people who listen before they speak.

Arthur looked at them.

"Is this..."

"Yes," Melisa said simply.

"How?"

"Long to explain. Five hundred years is time enough to find a way."

"Are you — Papa?" the girl asked.

Arthur opened his mouth. Closed it. Looked at Melisa. Melisa looked at him — with the expression he remembered from the first evening on the terrace by the sea. Quiet. Waiting.

Protocol Λ did not provide for children. But five hundred years is a very long time for the things that weren't provided for.

"Yes. I'm Papa."

* * *

He stood. Walked to the window. The earth beyond the glass was green to the horizon. In the sky were two suns.

"Are we on Earth?"

"Yes. And no."

"What has changed?"

She came over. Stood beside him. Shoulder to shoulder — as then, on that first terrace, three hundred-odd years ago.

"Everything. And nothing that matters."

"Do we need to go somewhere again?"

"We need to go somewhere again."

The boy came up to him from the other side. Took his hand — without asking, the way you take it when you consider it a matter of course. The hand was warm.

"What are their names?"

"Luca. And Sol."

Luce. Sun. Light — in two languages.

"I gave them everything while you slept. Now it's your turn."

Beyond the window the second sun began to sink below the horizon — slowly, solemnly, as if bidding farewell to all that had been before.

Arthur held the boy's warm hand. Looked at the unfamiliar green infinity. Felt beside him — a shoulder, warmth, a scent that had not changed in five hundred years.

Inside him — the same fire. The one that does not flicker in the wind.

He took the first step.

* * *

In the year 2566, on a planet that people called by different names — some Earth, others a New Beginning, others simply Home — a man awoke who remembered a sea of an impossibly blue color.

Beside him were three.

And ahead — everything.
