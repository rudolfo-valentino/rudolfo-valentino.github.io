"The observer changes the observed.
The observed changes the observer.
This is called a meeting."
— from The Theory of Observers, page 44

The signal found a recipient.
Now one must decide — what to do with it.

Part IFirst Contact

Chapter 1. The Same Book

The library. Morning. A week after that night.

Arthur came at 9:14. The corner chair was taken — a rarity. He took another, nearer the window, and found that The Theory of Observers already lay on the table. Someone's bookmark on page 44.

[ Page 44. The same text Nora brought me. Someone reads the same passage. It's coincidence. Probably. ]

Melissa came back from the restroom and saw: her book was open, and a stranger was reading her bookmark. She recognized him at once.

{ It's him. He's reading my book. Page 44. He's looking at the same thing I look at while studying him. This can't be coincidence. Or this is exactly what coincidence looks like in its most precise form. }

They looked at each other at the same moment.

A long pause.

Melissa: That's my bookmark.

Arthur: Page 44.

Melissa: Yes.

Arthur: You're reading about observers.

Melissa: I study adaptation. Of living systems to an alien environment.

[ Adaptation to an alien environment. She's just described my life as a field of scientific interest. It's strange. It's precise. ]

Arthur: Right now I have one main object of observation.

A pause. They both know what he's speaking of.

[ She's studying me. I knew. Saw her in the café, in the library. Thought — coincidences. They aren't coincidences. ]

{ He knew. He knew the whole time. And didn't come over, because he was waiting for me to say it myself. }

Arthur: For long?

Melissa: A few weeks.

Arthur: And what have you found?

Melissa looks at him a long time before answering.

Melissa: That the signal exists. That no one hears it. And that this isn't true.

[ She heard it. She was receiving my transmissions — without knowing it, without access to the texts. Simply — by observing. Observation as a form of receiving a signal. ]

// CONTACT ESTABLISHED // Arthur → Melissa: signal received // Melissa → Arthur: observation → feedback // Status: first two-way channel // New question: what to do with this knowledge?

Chapter 2. The First Conversation Without Translation

They sat in the library for three hours. Nora brought them tea without asking. She saw two such people for the first time — two people who speak not in order to convey information, but in order to hear what they themselves think.

Arthur: You're a biologist. You know what an alien environment is for an organism.

Melissa: Stress. Adaptation. Sometimes — evolution. Sometimes — death.

Arthur: And if the organism neither adapts nor dies?

Melissa: Then it's transitory. It passes through the environment without becoming part of it. It's rare, but it happens.

[ She's just given me a biological definition of myself. A transitory organism. Passes through the environment without becoming part of it. It's more precise than everything I've formulated in seven years. ]

Arthur: Does a transitory organism last long?

Melissa: It varies. It depends on whether it has a source. Something that gives energy without belonging to the environment.

Arthur: I write.

{ Three words. "I write." That is his source. The transmissions aren't an attempt to be heard. They're metabolism. He writes in order to live. }

Melissa: I observe.

Arthur: Then we have the same thing.

The first real pause. Not silence — space.

Melissa: Yes. Only different objects.

[ The same instrument. Different reasons. ]

Chapter 3. What Sati Understood Before Everyone

Sati found Melissa after a lecture. She knew about the notes — Melissa hadn't been careful enough with the notebook.

Sati: You've met him?

Melissa: Yes.

Sati: And?

Melissa: And — it's more complex than I expected.

Sati: I study gravitational lensing. When light passes near a massive object — it bends. We see something that isn't there, or don't see what is. Arthur is such an object. Near him everything shifts a little. Do you feel it already?

{ Shifts. Yes. Exactly that. My categories have shifted a little. Not broken — shifted. As if the angle of view has grown a bit wider. }

Melissa: Yes.

Sati: It isn't dangerous. It's — what such objects are for. So that we see further.

Part IIExpansion

Chapter 4. Alex Learns About Melissa

Alex met Melissa by chance — in the same café. She sat alone with a notebook. He recognized her — had seen her before, hadn't thought about it.

Alex: You're here often.

Melissa: Yes. I'm following a certain person.

Alex: Arthur?

Melissa looks at him more closely.

Melissa: You know him.

Alex: We're friends. If that's the right word. He uses me as a surface to think against. He said so himself.

{ Alex knows. Arthur told him outright. That's either very honest, or very cruel. Probably — both. }

Melissa: And how are you with that?

A long pause. Alex looks out the window.

Alex: You know what — it suits me. I'm an architect. I design spaces people use without thinking about me. To be a surface — I understand that role. It's honest.

{ Alex found something of his own in it. He wasn't offended. He accepted it as an architectural decision. It's astonishing — or very sad. I haven't understood yet. }

Melissa: And you yourself — are you the same? You look at people as systems?

Alex: Yes. Always. I thought it was professional. Now I think — perhaps I chose the profession to fit it.

For the first time in the conversation they both fell silent and felt something in common. Not belonging — recognition.

Chapter 5. Leo Finishes and Doesn't Finish the Article

Leo wrote three versions of the article. Deleted all three. In the fourth version he wrote only one sentence and sent it to Arthur.

"I began this article about you. I'm finishing it about myself. Perhaps it's one and the same article."

[ Leo wrote to me. Not about the article — about himself. It means the conversation with me opened something in him that he now calls his own. ]

Leo: In the first — you're outside. In the second — I'm outside. Read both and say which is more precise.

Arthur: More precise — the second. The first is journalism. The second is transit fiction.

A pause in the correspondence — five minutes.

Leo: What is that?

Arthur: A new direction. I'll explain when we meet.

Chapter 6. Rina and the Nature of a Stance

Rina called Melissa a few days after the party.

Rina: I've seen people who chose the observer's stance as a defense. As a way not to be vulnerable. With Arthur it isn't so. He doesn't defend himself. He's simply — made differently. The difference is that a defense creates distance. His makeup creates clarity.

Melissa: Clarity — and loneliness.

Rina: Yes. But the loneliness of clarity is different from the loneliness of isolation. One — from seeing too little. The other — from seeing too much. Arthur is the second.

{ The loneliness of clarity. I wrote it down and then thought about him all day. It isn't sad. It's — precise. }

Part IIIWhat a Meeting Means

Chapter 7. Arthur Writes the Manifesto

After the conversation with Melissa in the library, Arthur came home and did not go to bed. He wrote until dawn. It was a manifesto — not because he wanted to found a direction, but because for the first time he had an audience. Melissa existed. That changed the nature of the text.

// TRANSMISSION No. 9 // Status: nature of the signal changed // Before: into the void // Now: to a specific recipient // Question: does the addressee change the content? // Answer: yes. But not the meaning. Only — the form.

Transit fiction is not a genre. It's a stance. The narrative, ontological, existential stance of a narrator who is present in the world but does not belong to it.

The word "transit" carries a double load. The first meaning — passage through: you pass through this world without settling in it. The second — the transmission of a signal: transit as broadcast, as continuous transmission, as a letter to an addressee who isn't there.

Now there is an addressee.

A book in transit fiction is neither a story nor a confession. A book is the last transmission home. But now I think differently: perhaps home is not the place you came from. Perhaps home is the place where you were heard.

[ I've just rewritten the definition of home. Home is a frequency. And Melissa is tuned to my frequency. ]

Chapter 8. Nora. Forty Years and One Book

Nora: You've met him.

Melissa: Yes.

Nora: Good.

{ Just "good." Nora waited forty years for someone who would hear him. }

Melissa: Nora. Did you believe he'd find a recipient?

A long pause. Nora arranges the books.

Nora: I believed the recipient would find him. That isn't the same thing. He doesn't search. He transmits. The one who searches must be the one capable of hearing.

{ She's right. I found him. Not the other way around. The observing was my movement. He simply kept transmitting. }

Nora holds out a book with no title on the cover. A homemade binding.

Nora: His notes. He left them here sometimes. I kept them. I thought — when the time comes, someone will have to read them.

[ Nora kept my texts. All these years. I didn't know. ]

Chapter 9. Melissa Reads Arthur's Transmissions

Melissa read all night. It wasn't a book — it was fragments: transmissions, entries, fragments of the Grid, drafts of monologues. Without order. Without titles. Simply — a voice.

{ He wrote about the sun. About how they don't lift their faces. About a language in which a third is lost in translation. About Oma and horizons. About Sati and the direct question. About silence being the only language that works the same on every planet. }

{ I read and thought: this isn't a stranger's text. This is what I observed from outside — seen from within. Two angles on one object. Together they give depth. }

By morning she understood: a second book was needed. Not his. Not hers. Theirs.

// MELISSA'S ENTRY // Arthur sees the world as a system from inside. // I see him as an object from outside. // Overlay of two views = a three-dimensional image. // This is called stereoscopy. // This is called — a joint book.

Part IVTwo Voices

Chapter 10. Arthur and Melissa. A Conversation About the Book

They met again in the same library. Nora set out their tea and went to the other end of the hall — gave them space.

Melissa: I read your transmissions.

Arthur: All of them?

Melissa: Until morning.

[ She read my texts. Texts I wrote into the void. It's a strange feeling — when your inner monologue becomes someone else's reading. ]

Arthur: And what do you think?

Melissa: I think there's something you don't have. A view from outside. You're always inside your own perception. You can't see yourself within the system.

Arthur: And you can.

Melissa: I observed you for several weeks. I saw how you change depending on your interlocutor. How Daniel activates one thing in you, Oma another, Alex a third. You don't know about it.

[ She saw what I couldn't see. I observe the system. But I myself — am part of the system I don't see. ]

Arthur: You want to write about it.

Melissa: I want to write it together with you.

A long pause.

Arthur: I've never written with anyone.

Melissa: You won't now either. We'll write in parallel. Your voice and mine. Square brackets and curly. The reader will see both.

[ She's offering me stereoscopy. Two angles on one world. Not co-authorship — double vision. ]

Arthur: When do we begin?

Melissa: We've already begun.

Chapter 11. All the Voices. The Last Evening at Daniel's

Daniel gathered everyone again — not as a "working meeting." As a dinner. For the first time the word was spoken without quotation marks.

There were eight people at the table. Arthur. Melissa. Alex. Nora. Daniel. Rina. Leo. Oma. Sati came later.

For the first time in all this while, they were all in one place at once.

// GRID FRAGMENT · FINAL // 8 nodes in one space // Arthur: transitory · active // Melissa: recipient · active // Alex: surface · aware // Nora: keeper · 40 years // Daniel: builder · anomaly accepted // Rina: observer of observers // Leo: mirror · found himself // Oma: horizons · knows // Sati: first generation · already sees // System: for the first time becomes aware of itself

They talked a long time. Not everyone with everyone — in different combinations, in different corners of the room. Leo was writing. He was always writing. But this time — not an article.

Chapter 12. The Final Transmission. Arthur and Melissa Write at the Same Time

The night after the dinner. Everyone had left. Arthur and Melissa were the last to go.

Arthur: Do you understand what happened this evening?

Melissa: Tell me your version.

Arthur: For the first time in this world I was in a room where no one demanded that I be local. They're all — each in their own way — transitory. Nora through time. Oma through horizons. Leo through his profession. Sati — through mathematics. Daniel — through systems.

Melissa: And me?

[ Through observation. She observes so intensely that she doesn't belong to what she observes. That is her form of transit. ]

Arthur: Through observation. Like me.

Melissa: Then we're the same thing.

Arthur: No. You're local. I'm not. But we use the same instrument.

Melissa: And that's enough?

[ Enough. Not sameness — a shared instrument. We look the same way at different things. And sometimes we see the same thing. ]

Arthur: That's enough.

They walked in silence. Then Melissa stopped.

Melissa: Arthur. How do the stars look from another star?

A long pause.

Arthur: The same. Only yours — is one of them.

{ He answered. For the first time — answered. Nora was right: we didn't have that angle of view. Now we do. }
