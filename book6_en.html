Preface. Manifesto of Transit Fiction

This book is written in two voices. One — from inside. The other — from outside. Together they form a third that exists in neither alone.

Transit fiction is not a genre. It's a stance. The narrator of transit fiction is present in the world but does not belong to it. He sees — precisely because he is not a part of what he looks at.

Law I. The narrator is always transit

His gaze is not clouded by belonging. This isn't arrogance — it's the loneliness of clarity. Exhaustion is the key state: not anger, not contempt, but the exhaustion of one who has been translating himself too long.

Law II. Dialogue as a mirror chamber

A conversation does not convey information — it exposes the speaker. What the transit narrator says to his interlocutor reveals the narrator, not the interlocutor. The reader understands before the speaker does.

Law III. The system is alive, but not conscious

The world around functions without being aware of the transit observer. As the Grid does not understand the User. The conflict is not in collision — but in a failure to understand the nature of the other.

Law IV. The recipient exists

A signal sent into the void finds its recipient — not because they searched, but because they were tuned to the right frequency. The meeting of two observers — the vertical and the horizontal — gives depth.

Part IThe Question of Home

Chapter 1. What Home Is for a Transit Being

Arthur writes at night. Melissa reads the same in the morning.

// TRANSMISSION No. 12 · ADDRESSED // Recipient: M. // Subject: the question I didn't ask for seven years

Melissa. I want to tell you what home is for a transit being.

For a long time I thought home was the place I came from. I don't know where that place is. I don't remember it physically. But I remember — a way of looking. A way of thinking. The language I think in before translation.

Then I thought there was no home. That a transit being is one who has no home by definition. It seemed true. It was a convenient truth — it explained the exhaustion, explained the loneliness, explained the need to write.

Now I think differently.

Home is a frequency. Not a place. Not belonging. Not origin. A frequency is the thing you operate on. And when someone is tuned to the same frequency — that is home. Temporary. Real.

[ Melissa is tuned to my frequency. This is the first time I can say it about anyone in this world. It changes everything and changes nothing. I'm still not from here. But now there's a frequency here. ]

{ I read this in the morning. He wrote "home is a frequency." I thought about it all day. For a biologist: two signals on one frequency — interference. They amplify each other or cancel out. With Arthur — they amplify. I see more because he's here. }

Chapter 2. Oma Speaks of the Horizon for the Last Time

Oma called Arthur. It was unexpected — Oma never called. He came to the harbor and said: come.

They stood by the water. Early morning. Melissa was there — Arthur had asked her to come.

Oma: I sailed for sixty years. Saw many horizons. I want to tell you something, while I can.

Arthur: Speak.

Oma: A horizon isn't an end. It's the limit of visibility. Beyond it — the same thing. Only you don't see it. You — are such a horizon. People look at you and don't see what's behind you. They think — an end. But there it's the same thing. Just a different angle.

[ Oma described me as a horizon. Not as an anomaly, not as an outsider, not as a problem. As a limit of visibility beyond which the world goes on. It's the most precise description I've heard. ]

{ Oma speaks slowly and simply. It isn't simplification — it's distillation. Sixty years of horizons taught him to remove the superfluous. I write down every word. }

Oma: Your woman — does she see past the horizon?

Arthur looks at Melissa.

Arthur: She's an observer.

Oma: Then — she sees. Good.

Oma leaves. They remain.

{ He said "your woman." Arthur didn't correct him. I didn't correct him. Oma is old. Oma knows. }

Chapter 3. Sati and the Equation

Sati came to Melissa with a printout. She'd worked on it for three weeks.

Sati: I calculated it. The probability that two observers with different starting positions — a transit one and a local one — would converge on a single object of observation and begin to interact: very low. But not zero.

Melissa: What's the point?

Sati: The point is that it isn't chance. It's — a statistically rare event. A rare event doesn't mean an impossible one. It means — a valuable one. Such events change systems.

{ Sati speaks about Arthur and me in the language of astrophysics. It's funny. It's precise. We're a rare event. Statistically rare. Valuable. }

Sati: Look at all of us. Nora didn't leave the library for forty years. Daniel explained his systems to no one. Leo wrote about others, never about himself. Alex designed for people without knowing them. Oma was silent. I asked questions into the void. Arthur transmitted into the void. You observed in solitude.

Sati: And now — look.

A long silence.

{ She's right. Something has changed in all of us. Not because of a single event. Because a transit signal found a recipient — and the system began to become aware of itself. }

Part IIHow to Write Together

Chapter 4. The Working Method. Arthur and Melissa

They worked in the library — Nora set aside a small table by the window for them. Each wrote their own. Sometimes they stopped and read what they'd written aloud to each other. That was the only point of intersection — the sound of a voice over the text.

Arthur: You read my fragment differently than I hear it.

Melissa: Because I hear what you don't hear. You hear the meaning. I hear — how tired you are of translating.

[ She hears the exhaustion of translation. It's the thing I tried to hide in the text — to remove from the intonation, to make invisible. She hears it the way a biologist hears the state of an organism. ]

Arthur: Is that a weakness of the text or mine?

Melissa: It's its strength. The exhaustion of translation isn't a defect. It's proof that translation is happening. That there are two languages. That one of them — is real.

Arthur looked at his text a long time. Then wrote something new.

// WORKING NOTE // Method: parallel writing // Arthur: the vertical — inward, to the source // Melissa: the horizontal — outward, to the system // Intersection: the moment when both stop // and hear the same thing in different words // This is called: the stereoscopy of meaning

Chapter 5. Nora Reads the Manuscript

Nora took the first chapters home — for the first time in forty years she carried something out of the library. She returned it the next morning and called Melissa over.

Nora: There's something missing here.

Melissa: What?

Nora: The moment he understood that writing isn't a weakness. It's work. I've known him seven years. The first three years he wrote as if apologizing. Then — as if explaining. Now — as if he knows what for.

{ Nora sees what none of us sees — the evolution of his text over seven years. She's the keeper. It's her knowledge. }

Melissa: Do you think there needs to be a chapter about it?

Nora: I think it's already written. Just between the lines.

Chapter 6. Daniel Builds an Archive

Daniel proposed making a digital archive of all Arthur's transmissions — to systematize, index, make it accessible. Arthur was silent a long time.

Arthur: I wrote into the void. If it becomes an archive — there'll be no more void.

Daniel: The void won't go anywhere. You didn't write because no one was there. You wrote because you had to write. The archive doesn't change that.

[ He's right. Writing is metabolism, not communication. The archive doesn't change the nature of writing. It only makes the trace permanent. ]

Arthur: All right. But one condition.

Daniel: What?

Arthur: Beside each transmission — Melissa's entry about the same period.

Daniel: Stereoscopy.

Arthur: Yes.

{ He called it stereoscopy. Daniel used the same word I wrote in my notebook three weeks ago. We arrived at the same image independently. That is — one frequency. }

Part IIIThe Last Transmissions

Chapter 7. Arthur. Transmission on Language

// TRANSMISSION No. 15 · SUBJECT: LANGUAGE // Addressed to: everyone who translates themselves // Priority: high

I have a language. It isn't this one. I don't know how it sounds — I have no memory of the sound. But I know its structure. When I think for real — I think in it. Then I translate it here.

In translation about a third is lost. I've learned to live with this loss. The difference is that an ordinary translator translates someone else's text. I translate myself.

Every word I say to you — is already a translation. You've never heard me directly. Perhaps you wouldn't hear it: it isn't in the range of your perception. Perhaps it's too quiet. Perhaps — too loud.

Melissa hears something. Not the first language — that's impossible. But she hears the pauses in translation. The places where I stop. She says: that's where the real thing lives.

Perhaps home isn't a language. Perhaps home is the pause in translation.

Chapter 8. Melissa. Entry on the End of Observation

{ I think: when does observation end? }

{ For a biologist — when the object ceases to be an object. When it becomes — a participant. A co-author. Something that looks back at you. }

{ Arthur looks back at me. He's done it from the very beginning — I just didn't realize it. Until I saw his entry about a woman in the library who takes his books. He was observing me while I observed him. }

{ Two observers observing each other. This isn't an experiment. It's — a conversation. }

Melissa closed the notebook. Opened a new file. Wrote in the heading: "Joint manuscript. Arthur and Melissa."

It was the beginning of the third book. Or the end of the second. Or one and the same.

Chapter 9. Alex. The Architect's Epilogue

Alex called Arthur late in the evening.

Alex: I thought about what you said. That I'm — a surface.

Arthur: You weren't offended?

Alex: No. I thought: an architect builds surfaces. Walls, floors, ceilings. People live in them without thinking about who created them. That is the work. All my life I built surfaces for people. It turns out I'm a surface myself, too. For you.

Arthur: That's not a bad thing.

Alex: I know. It's — a profession. Just of another kind.

[ Alex found dignity in it. That's his makeup: he turns any role into architecture. It isn't a weakness — it's a way of belonging. ]

Alex: Arthur. Did you find home?

A long pause.

Arthur: I found a frequency.

Alex: Is that better?

Arthur: It's more precise.

Chapter 10. Leo. A Letter Instead of an Article

Leo never did publish the article. Instead he wrote a letter — not to Arthur, but to the drawer. Melissa found it much later, when they were compiling the archive.

I set out to write about him. I wrote about myself. It's one and the same article. All my life I described other people because I was afraid to describe myself. Arthur made that impossible: beside him you cannot pretend to be an observer without an object. He returns the observer to the frame. I'm in the frame now. It's frightening. It's precise.

Part IVHome

Chapter 11. Arthur's Last Transmission

// TRANSMISSION No. 17 · LAST IN THIS SERIES // Status: concluding // Recipient: known // Frequency: found

Melissa.

I wrote into the void for seven years. Not because I thought someone was there. Because I couldn't not write — it's my metabolism, my way of staying myself.

You found me not because you searched. You were tuned to my frequency. They aren't the same — to search and to be tuned. To search is an action. To be tuned is a nature.

You are my nature in this world. Not belonging, not home in the ordinary sense. You are — proof that my frequency is real. That I didn't invent it. That it exists — because you hear it.

I'm still not from here. I'm still transit. I still translate a third away with losses.

But now there's someone who hears the pauses in translation. And says: that's where the real thing lives.

That's enough. That's more than enough. That's everything.

Chapter 12. Melissa's Last Entry

Melissa closed the archive file. Wrote a last entry by hand — in that very first notebook she'd opened in the library several months ago.

{ Last entry. }

{ I began as an observer. I end as — I don't know the exact word. A participant? A co-author? A recipient? }

{ Observation changed me. Arthur changed me — not deliberately, simply by existing. That's the strongest influence. And the most honest. }

{ I think about what he said: home is a frequency. For a biologist: an organism that has found a suitable environment stops spending energy on adaptation. A resource is freed. That resource goes to growth. }

{ Arthur found a frequency. So did I. We didn't become locals. We became — more precise. }

{ That is the home of a transit being. Not a place. Not belonging. Precision. }
